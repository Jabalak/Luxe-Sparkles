Front Matter

Declaration & Statement of Approach

This dissertation is submitted in partial fulfilment of the requirements of the degree programme named on the title page. It is my own work and has not been submitted elsewhere.

Nature of this study

The study is a desk-based, theory-building dissertation. Its evidence base is published literature and secondary data only (peer-reviewed journal articles, book chapters, industry and government statistics, certification registers and corporate sustainability disclosures covering 2021–2026, with selected foundational works). No primary human-subjects data were collected.

Where the text reports figures, they are attributed to their source in the reference list. Where the study advances propositions, they are presented as conceptual propositions for future empirical testing, not as measured causal effects.

Why a secondary-data design is appropriate here

Digital leadership, service innovation and Industry 4.0 adoption in luxury island resorts are fast-moving phenomena. A rigorous synthesis of the last five years of research, combined with national tourism statistics and hospitality-industry disclosures, allows the study to (i) consolidate a fragmented literature, (ii) situate it in a specific and under-researched island destination, and (iii) produce a testable framework and a set of managerial and policy recommendations — without the access, cost and ethical burden of a multi-property primary survey.

A transparent account of the design, search protocol, inclusion criteria and trustworthiness procedures is given in Chapter 3 (Methodology). Limitations are discussed in Chapter 6.

Contributions

Theoretical: an integrated competency–capability–innovation framework. Contextual: the first synthesis positioned on Mauritian luxury beach resorts. Practical: a diagnostic and roadmap for resort leadership teams.

Audience

Resort general managers and HR/talent leaders, destination managers and the Mauritius Tourism Promotion Authority, hospitality educators, and researchers in digital transformation and sustainable tourism.

Front Matter

Abstract

Luxury beach resorts in Mauritius confront two simultaneous pressures: the imperative to adopt Industry 4.0 technologies, and the obligation to innovate services sustainably — protecting the very natural and cultural assets on which luxury depends. This dissertation asks what role digital leadership competencies play in driving sustainable service innovation in this context.

Using a desk-based design grounded in literature and secondary data published between 2021 and 2026, the study synthesises research on digital leadership, service innovation, sustainability in hospitality and Industry 4.0 adoption; triangulates it with Mauritian tourism statistics, hotel-sector studies and resort sustainability disclosures; and analyses the combined evidence through reflexive thematic analysis.

The synthesis converges on five digital leadership competency clusters — (1) Digital vision and strategic foresight, (2) Digital literacy and data-driven decision-making, (3) Agile, adaptive change leadership, (4) Innovation and entrepreneurial orientation, and (5) Ethical, responsible and sustainable digital stewardship. These competencies act as microfoundations of organisational dynamic capabilities (sensing, seizing, transforming) that, in turn, enable sustainable service innovation — across service-experience, process, business-model and social-ecological dimensions. Industry 4.0 technologies (IoT and smart-room systems, AI and personalisation, big-data revenue and guest analytics, cloud platforms and contactless service, service robotics, and digital sustainability-monitoring platforms) are enabling mechanisms, while guest technology acceptance, workforce digital skills, legacy systems, cost and data-protection obligations act as moderating conditions.

The study offers a refined conceptual framework and six propositions linking digital leadership competencies to sustainable service innovation in luxury beach resorts, contextualised to Mauritius’s distinctive island economy, dependency on aviation and upmarket guest segments, and its biodiversity and climate vulnerabilities. It concludes with managerial and policy recommendations and a research agenda for primary empirical testing.

Keywords: digital leadership; sustainable service innovation; Industry 4.0; luxury hospitality; Mauritius; dynamic capabilities; digital transformation; island tourism.

Chapter One

1Introduction

1.1 Background to the study

Two forces are reshaping luxury hospitality at the same time: the digitisation of every part of the guest journey, and the demand that tourism become sustainable rather than merely less damaging.

The fourth industrial revolution — commonly termed Industry 4.0 — describes the fusion of physical operations with cyber-physical systems, cloud computing, the Internet of Things (IoT), big data, artificial intelligence (AI), robotics and blockchain. In hospitality it has produced what scholars increasingly call Hotel 4.0 or Tourism 4.0: a service environment in which sensors, platforms and algorithms mediate the guest experience, support decision-making and coordinate operations across distributed resort properties (Buhalis, Efthymiou, Uzunboylu & Thrassou, 2024; The implications of Industry 4.0 for the tourism sector, 2024). For luxury beach resorts, this is not simply a technology procurement question. It changes how value is created, how service quality is evidenced, and how personalisation at scale becomes possible.

Simultaneously, the hospitality industry’s sustainability agenda has moved from a peripheral marketing theme to a core strategic concern. Bibliometric reviews of the last five years show sustainability scholarship in hotels and restaurants expanding rapidly, with environmental, social and governance (ESG) framing increasingly embedded in competitiveness and finance debates (Blanco-Moreno et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2026). Sustainable tourism is no longer framed as a constraint on luxury but as a condition of its long-term licence to operate — particularly for island destinations whose product is their environment.

Mauritius is a paradigmatic case. A small island economy in the Indian Ocean, it built a successful upmarket tourism sector around luxury beach resorts, lagoons, coral reefs and an appealing cultural mix. Tourism is a mainstay of the economy, and after the near-collapse of international travel during the COVID-19 pandemic the sector has recovered strongly: according to Statistics Mauritius, tourist arrivals rose from 1,382,177 in 2024 to 1,436,250 in 2025 — an increase of 3.9% — with gross tourism earnings reported at approximately Rs 93.6 billion in 2024 (Statistics Mauritius, 2026; Marques, 2026). Yet the destination faces the same structural exposures as other island economies: dependence on long-haul aviation and a handful of source markets, climate and coral-reef risk, water and waste pressures, and intensifying regional competition.

Digital transformation and sustainability are therefore converging precisely where leadership decisions are made. Yet the two literatures have largely developed in parallel. Studies of digital leadership propose competency sets for executives steering digital change (Ongena, 2024; Schiuma et al., 2024; López-Figueroa et al., 2025). Studies of service innovation examine how firms renew their offerings, processes and business models, with a growing sustainability orientation (Elkhwesky et al., 2022; Ziyae & Sadeghi, 2021; Figueiredo et al., 2026). Relatively few works connect the two — and fewer still do so in a luxury island-resort setting. That intersection is the subject of this dissertation.

1.2 Problem statement

Luxury beach resorts in Mauritius must innovate their services to remain competitive in an Industry 4.0 environment while simultaneously making those innovations environmentally and socially sustainable. Both requirements place exceptional demands on organisational leadership: they require the ability to sense weak signals (changing guest expectations, new technologies, emerging regulation, environmental limits), to seize opportunities through investment and service redesign, and to transform the organisation so that new practices stick. These are the classic components of the dynamic capabilities view (Teece, 2007), and they are increasingly theorised as leadership-shaped microfoundations (Schiuma et al., 2024; de la Torre et al., 2025).

The problem this study addresses is threefold. First, the digital leadership competency literature is fragmented and predominantly generic — it is rarely translated into the operating realities of a resort, where high-touch personal service, thin margins and seasonal demand coexist with new technology. Second, the sustainable service innovation literature rarely foregrounds leadership as the enabling mechanism; innovation is often treated as a firm-level outcome without specifying who supplies the agency. Third, the Mauritian luxury beach resort context — a distinctive island setting facing acute environmental and structural pressures — is under-represented in this international literature, so practitioners lack contextually grounded guidance.

Because the field is fast-moving and dispersed, a literature-based, secondary-data synthesis is the appropriate first step: it consolidates what the last five years of scholarship can tell us, positions it in the Mauritian context, and produces a framework and propositions precise enough to be tested empirically.

1.3 Research questions

The study is organised around one overarching question and three supporting questions.

Overarching research question

How do digital leadership competencies drive sustainable service innovation in luxury beach resorts in Mauritius facing Industry 4.0?

RQ1. What digital leadership competencies does the recent literature (2021–2026) identify as salient for driving innovation and sustainability in service organisations, and in hospitality specifically?
RQ2. How do Industry 4.0 technologies enable sustainable service innovation in luxury hospitality, and through what mechanisms?
RQ3. What does available secondary evidence reveal about the digital-transformation and sustainability profile of Mauritian luxury beach resorts, and the pressures they face?
RQ4. How can digital leadership competencies, Industry 4.0 enablers and sustainable service innovation be integrated into a coherent conceptual framework for this context, and what propositions does it yield?

1.4 Research objectives

  1. To identify and synthesise digital leadership competency frameworks from recent scholarship and distil them into a coherent set of competency clusters (RQ1).
  2. To examine how Industry 4.0 technologies function as enabling mechanisms for sustainable service innovation in luxury hospitality (RQ2).
  3. To assemble and interpret secondary evidence on the Mauritian luxury beach resort sector’s digital and sustainability profile (RQ3).
  4. To develop an integrated conceptual framework and a set of testable propositions linking digital leadership competencies to sustainable service innovation in this context (RQ4).
  5. To derive managerial and policy recommendations and a future research agenda.

1.5 Rationale & significance

Theoretical significance. The study connects two bodies of work — digital leadership and sustainable service innovation — through the dynamic capabilities lens, proposing that leadership competencies operate as microfoundations of the sensing–seizing–transforming cycle. This responds to explicit calls in the literature for work on the managerial enablers of service innovation and for research that integrates sustainability and digitalisation rather than treating them separately (Buhalis et al., 2024; Blanco-Moreno et al., 2025).

Contextual significance. Mauritius is an instructive but under-studied setting. Its luxury resorts are internationally benchmarked, its sustainability pressures are acute, and its policy environment — including the Mauritius Declaration on Digitalisation and Sustainable Tourism (UNWTO, 2018) and successive national budgets promoting sustainable tourism — explicitly couples the two themes. Yet there is little synthesised guidance on the leadership competencies that would operationalise that coupling.

Practical significance. The framework and diagnostic implied by the study can support resort leadership teams in auditing their own competencies, prioritising technology investments and building sustainability into innovation rather than bolting it on. For educators and professional bodies, the competency clusters offer a basis for curriculum and development design. For destination managers, the analysis highlights collective-action needs — skills, data infrastructure and certification — that individual resorts cannot solve alone.

1.6 Scope & delimitations

In scope: the large (4- and 5-star, and “luxury collection”) beach resorts of Mauritius and their leadership cadres; digital leadership competencies as reported in scholarly and credible practitioner literature; Industry 4.0 technologies applicable to resort service delivery; sustainable service innovation as a multi-dimensional construct; and secondary data on the Mauritian tourism and hospitality sector.

Out of scope: primary data collection (surveys, interviews, observation); non-beach and non-luxury accommodation (e.g., guesthouses, city hotels) except where used for contrast; detailed technical engineering of specific systems; and quantification of financial returns, which would require firm-level primary data.

Delimitations. The evidence window is primarily 2021–2026, with foundational works cited where a concept’s lineage matters. The unit of analysis is the resort leadership and the organisation, not the individual guest, though guest technology acceptance is treated as a moderating condition.

Chapter Two

2Literature Review

This chapter reviews the literature underpinning the study across four domains — digital leadership, service innovation and its sustainability turn, Industry 4.0 in luxury hospitality, and the Mauritian context — then integrates them into a theoretical and conceptual framework.

2.1 Digital leadership: from e-leadership to Leader 4.0

Leadership research has repeatedly adapted to technological shifts. Early notions of e-leadership described leading distributed, technology-mediated teams; later treatments of Leadership 4.0 repositioned the leader as the orchestrator of socio-technical change in the Industry 4.0 era (Oberer & Erkollar, 2018). Over the last five years the construct has consolidated around digital leadership: the capability to set digital direction, cultivate a digital culture, make evidence-based decisions and renew the organisation continuously (López-Figueroa et al., 2025; Salamzadeh et al., 2023).

Two points recur in this literature. First, digital leadership is not merely technical proficiency; it is a blend of vision, culture-shaping and change capability in which technology is the medium rather than the message. Second, the construct is increasingly tied to organisational outcomes — transformation success, agility and innovation — rather than to individual style preferences (Schiuma et al., 2024; Hruška, 2024).

2.2 Digital leadership competencies

A competency is a combination of knowledge, skills, abilities and behaviours that enables effective performance. Recent reviews and frameworks converge on a broadly similar architecture, although labels differ. Ongena (2024), drawing on the European e-Competence Framework, identifies five main competencies crucial to managers leading digital transformation. Schiuma et al. (2024) distinguish six “transformative” competencies that characterise leaders who drive successful organisational transformation. López-Figueroa et al. (2025) find communication, trust and team cohesion to be among the most impactful factors in digital leadership, while Imran et al. (2020) propose digital vision, digital knowledge, failing fast, empowerment and managing diverse teams. In hospitality specifically, the EHL hospitality-leadership work identities a large sub-skill set for an AI-driven future, and Ongena-type frameworks have been applied to service managers.

Synthesising these, five competency clusters recur (detailed and evidenced in Chapter 4):

  1. Digital vision & strategic foresight — articulating where digital and sustainability agendas meet, and translating that into a compelling destination.
  2. Digital literacy & data-driven decision-making — fluency with data, analytics and emerging technologies sufficient to interrogate and direct them.
  3. Agile, adaptive change leadership — experimenting, failing fast, and bringing the workforce and stakeholders along.
  4. Innovation & entrepreneurial orientation — spotting and scaling new service, process and business-model opportunities.
  5. Ethical, responsible & sustainable digital stewardship — stewardship of data, people and natural assets as part of the digital mandate.

2.3 Service innovation & its sustainability turn

Service innovation refers to the introduction of new or significantly improved services, service processes, or service business models. A comprehensive review of technology-driven service innovation in hospitality organises the field around new service offerings, customer interfaces, delivery systems and enabling technologies (Kaushal & Srivastava, 2023, as reviewed in the IJCHM synthesis). The dynamic-capabilities view has proven especially productive: Ziyae and Sadeghi (2021) conceptualise hotel service innovation explicitly through dynamic capabilities, and more recent work finds dynamic capabilities driving marketing agility and service innovation, and service-innovation capability mediating the link between dynamic capabilities and sustainable competitive advantage (Figueiredo et al., 2026; Ziyae & Sadeghi, 2021).

The sustainability turn has been decisive. Elkhwesky et al. (2022) provided the first systematic, critical review of sustainable innovation in hospitality and tourism, identifying environmental, social and economic drivers and consequences. Subsequent bibliometric work documents the rapid expansion of the field and a shift from standalone “green practices” toward integrated ESG and circular-economy logics (Blanco-Moreno et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2026). Related work links green environmental orientation to green service innovation and sustainable development outcomes, and finds that quality and sustainability practices together raise guest satisfaction and destination loyalty (AL Mushattat et al., 2025; Vargová Dzurov et al., 2025). Open-innovation studies similarly show that collaboration improves service innovation in tourism (Pinhal et al., 2025).

2.4 Industry 4.0 in luxury hospitality

A systematic review of Industry 4.0 implications for tourism documents the digital paradigm shift and its organisational consequences, including new capability requirements (The implications of Industry 4.0 for the tourism sector, 2024). Buhalis et al. (2024) chart technology adoption in tourism and hospitality and emphasise that value is realised through organisational and leadership adaptation, not technology alone. Work on hospitality technology adoption also identifies entry barriers alongside added value (Navigating the Technological Landscape in Hospitality, 2024). Research specific to luxury finds digital transformation to be a strategic imperative for the sector’s future competitiveness and profitability (Is the digitalisation the future of the luxury industry?, 2024).

On the demand side, acceptance is not automatic. Meta-UTAUT work on guest acceptance of service robots and TAM/SOR studies of smart-hotel technology show that perceived usefulness, ease of use, hedonic value and resistance all shape adoption (Marghany et al., 2025; Yang et al.; “Young luxury hotel guests…”, 2025). Technology-acceptance antecedents have been examined for digital transformation in star-rated hotels (Jayawardena et al., 2023). This matters acutely for luxury, where high-touch human service is itself the product.

2.5 The Mauritian luxury beach resort context

Mauritius is a small island developing state whose tourism sector is built on an upmarket, resort-based model. After the pandemic shock, arrivals recovered to approximately 1.38 million in 2024 and 1.44 million in 2025, with gross tourism earnings around Rs 93.6 billion in 2024 (Statistics Mauritius, 2026; Marques, 2026). National data also show continued year-on-year growth into 2025 and 2026, alongside downside risks linked to source-market concentration and aviation capacity (Fitch Solutions/BMI, 2025; Tourism Analytics, 2026).

The academic literature on Mauritius hospitality remains comparatively thin but is growing. Tandrayen-Ragoobur (2025) examines how the Mauritian hotel industry managed uncertainty, highlighting digital transformation, social-media marketing, restructuring and innovation as adaptive strategies. Ramsamy (2024) assesses quality-management principles supporting sustainable development in the Mauritian hospitality sector, noting the sustainable practices of major local groups such as Beachcomber and LUX Resorts. Industry evidence corroborates this: Beachcomber Resorts & Hotels achieved EarthCheck Gold certification (2023), and member hotels deploy recycled-water irrigation, glass instead of plastic, and energy-optimising measures as part of the destination’s green-certification ambitions (Beachcomber Resorts & Hotels; WTTC, 2023).

Structural and contextual factors shape any digital-sustainability agenda here: a high dependence on long-haul aviation and a few source markets; biodiversity and coral-reef vulnerability; water and waste pressures on a small island; a labour market with skills gaps in digital roles; and a regulatory environment including the Data Protection Act that constrains how guest data may be used. Policy has long recognised the coupling of digitalisation and sustainability, from the Mauritius Declaration on Digitalisation and Sustainable Tourism (UNWTO, 2018) to recent budgets promoting sustainable tourism development.

2.6 Theoretical framing

The study is anchored in the dynamic capabilities view, supplemented by the resource-based view, technology-acceptance theory and diffusion theory.

Dynamic capabilities (primary lens). Teece’s (2007) framework describes how firms integrate, build and reconfigure competences to address rapidly changing environments, through the capacities to sense opportunities and threats, seize them through investment and business-model design, and transform — continuously renewing the organisation. Recent work explicitly connects dynamic capabilities to digital transformation and digital innovation (Janssen et al., 2021; de la Torre et al., 2025), and to service innovation and hotel performance (Ziyae & Sadeghi, 2021; Figueiredo et al., 2026). Crucially, Schiuma et al. (2024) position leadership competencies as a driver of transformation success — i.e., as microfoundations of the firm’s sensing–seizing–transforming cycle. This is the hinge of the present study: digital leadership competencies are treated as the human microfoundations of dynamic capabilities, which in turn enable sustainable service innovation.

Resource-based view. Rare, valuable, inimitable resources sustain advantage. Leadership competence and a digitally enabled, sustainability-oriented culture are candidate resources that are hard to imitate, supporting the argument that digital leadership is a source of durable differentiation rather than a one-off project.

Technology-acceptance theory (TAM/UTAUT). Because technology only creates value if guests and staff use it, acceptance frameworks explain the moderating conditions under which Industry 4.0 investments translate into service innovation (Venkatesh et al., 2003; Marghany et al., 2025; Jayawardena et al., 2023).

Diffusion of innovation. Rogers’ (2003) model helps explain why adoption diffuses unevenly across resort properties and why leadership champions matter at the point of adoption.

2.8 Research gap & synthesis

The review reveals four gaps. First, digital leadership competency frameworks are rarely translated into hospitality operating contexts, and even more rarely into luxury island resorts. Second, sustainability and digitalisation literatures remain largely separate, despite both being central to competitive advantage. Third, leadership is under-specified as the agency behind service innovation; dynamic-capabilities work often stops at firm-level capabilities without naming the human competencies that build them. Fourth, Mauritius is under-represented, so contextual guidance is scarce. This study addresses these gaps through a synthesis that (i) consolidates competency dimensions, (ii) links them to dynamic capabilities, (iii) adds sustainable service innovation as the outcome, and (iv) situates the whole in the Mauritian luxury beach-resort context.

Chapter Three

3Methodology

This chapter states the research philosophy and approach, explains the desk-based design, specifies the search protocol and data sources, and describes how the evidence was analysed and quality assured.

3.1 Research philosophy & approach

The study adopts a pragmatic–interpretivist stance. It is pragmatic in recognising that the research question is exploratory and theory-building, and that the most appropriate evidence is the accumulated body of recent scholarship plus credible secondary data. It is interpretivist in accepting that competency labels and meanings are socially constructed and that synthesis requires judgement rather than purely mechanical aggregation.

The approach is inductive–abductive: it begins inductively by clustering the competency and innovation themes observed across sources, and then abductively connects them to an established theoretical lens (dynamic capabilities) to explain why those competencies should matter. This mirrors the logic of a conceptual/review-based dissertation, which advances theory and testable propositions rather than estimating effects.

3.2 Research design

The design is a desk-based systematic literature synthesis combined with secondary-data analysis. Two evidence streams were combined:

  1. Stream A — scholarly literature (2021–2026, with foundational works): peer-reviewed articles and reviews on digital leadership, service innovation, sustainability in hospitality, Industry 4.0 and technology acceptance.
  2. Stream B — secondary/contextual data: national statistics and central-bank tourism data for Mauritius, industry and destination reports, plurality-positioned hospitality studies on Mauritius, and resort-group sustainability/certification disclosures.

Triangulating the two streams allows the study to move from generic constructs (Stream A) to a context-specific framework (Stream B) — a recognised strength of desk-based designs where several independent sources converge.

3.3 Search strategy & inclusion criteria

3.5 Analytical procedure

Combined evidence was analysed using reflexive thematic analysis following the recognised six-phase approach — familiarisation, initial coding, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report (Braun & Clarke, 2006; updated as reflexive TA). Coding was a mix of inductive (open) and deductive (framework-driven) coding, the latter informed by dynamic capabilities and the competency dimensions identified in Chapter 2.

For the numeric secondary data, simple descriptive analysis was applied: year-on-year change in arrivals and earnings, recovery relative to the pre-pandemic baseline, and market concentration. No inferential statistical modelling was performed, consistent with the desk-based design.

The six themes that emerged are reported in Chapter 4 and then mapped back to the research questions in Chapter 5.

3.6 Quality & trustworthiness

Because the study is not empirical in the survey sense, validity is addressed through trustworthiness criteria (Lincoln & Guba, 1985):

  • Credibility — triangulation across scholarly and secondary sources; sourcing of numeric claims to official data.
  • Transferability — thick description of the Mauritian context so readers can judge applicability elsewhere.
  • Dependability — a documented, replicable search protocol with explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria.
  • Confirmability — propositions presented as interpretations, with the evidence base made transparent and the researcher’s prior framing acknowledged.

Two additional safeguards were applied. First, the PRISMA-style discipline of stating the search blocks and selection criteria supports replicability (Page et al., 2021). Second, the distinction between reported evidence (what sources say) and interpretation (what the study infers) is maintained throughout.

3.7 Ethics & limitations

Ethics. The study uses no primary human-subjects data, so no consent, anonymity or vulnerable-participant procedures are engaged. Ethical obligations nonetheless apply at the level of scholarship: accurate attribution of sources, honest representation of findings, respect for corporate disclosures, and avoidance of fabricated or unverifiable claims. All numeric claims are attributed to a source; conceptual claims are framed as interpretations or propositions.

Methodological limitations. A desk-based synthesis cannot measure the actual competency levels of Mauritian resort executives, cannot establish causality, and depends on the quality and availability of secondary sources. Publication and English-language bias are possible, and corporate disclosures may be selectively positive. These are acknowledged as limitations in Chapter 6 and motivate the future empirical agenda set out there.

Chapter Four

4Findings & Analysis

Six themes emerged from the synthesis. Each is presented with its supporting evidence, followed by the study’s interpretation. Reported facts are attributed; interpretations are marked as such.

4.1 Theme 1 — The digital leadership competency landscape

Across recent frameworks, five competency clusters recur with high consistency, even where labels and granularities differ (Ongena, 2024; Schiuma et al., 2024; López-Figueroa et al., 2025; Imran et al., 2020).

  1. Digital vision & strategic foresight. The ability to articulate a compelling digital future and align it with business and sustainability strategy. Schiuma et al. (2024) show that transformative leaders balance idealistic vision with pragmatic execution — relevant to resorts where a bold vision must survive seasonal cash-flow realities.
  2. Digital literacy & data-driven decision-making. Fluency sufficient to interrogate analytics and direct technology investments. Ongena’s (2024) e-CF-based framework places socio-technical integration at the centre of the manager’s role.
  3. Agile, adaptive change leadership. The capacity to experiment, learn quickly (“failing fast” in Imran et al., 2020) and mobilise people. López-Figueroa et al. (2025) identify communication, trust and team cohesion as dominant factors.
  4. Innovation & entrepreneurial orientation. Spotting and scaling new service and business-model opportunities — the bridge to service innovation.
  5. Ethical, responsible & sustainable digital stewardship. Stewardship of guest data, of the workforce and of natural assets — increasingly explicit in leadership discourse as ESG and digital ethics converge (Salamzadeh et al., 2023; Shin et al., 2026).

4.2 Theme 2 — Competencies as microfoundations of dynamic capabilities

The most consistent theoretical finding is that leadership competencies do not act directly on innovation outcomes; they build the organisational capabilities through which innovation happens. Mapping the clusters onto Teece’s (2007) sensing–seizing–transforming cycle shows a clean correspondence (interpretation):

  • Sensing draws on digital literacy and data-driven decision-making, plus strategic foresight — the firm’s ability to detect changing guest expectations, new technologies, environmental limits and regulatory shifts.
  • Seizing draws on innovation and entrepreneurial orientation plus agile change leadership — converting signals into investments, pilots and redesigned services.
  • Transforming draws on agile change leadership and ethical, sustainable stewardship — embedding new practices, re-aligning culture and renewing the organisation continously.

This aligns with recent work positioning leadership competencies as drivers of transformation success (Schiuma et al., 2024) and with evidence that dynamic capabilities drive marketing agility, service innovation and sustained competitive advantage in hotels (Figueiredo et al., 2026; Ziyae & Sadeghi, 2021; de la Torre et al., 2025). It also responds to the review-level observation that technology adoption in tourism delivers value through organisational adaptation (Buhalis et al., 2024).

4.3 Theme 3 — Industry 4.0 enablers in luxury resorts

The literature and industry evidence describe a layered technology stack. Value arises not from any single technology but from integration across the layers (Buhalis et al., 2024; The implications of Industry 4.0 for the tourism sector, 2024).

Two findings qualify this. First, demand-side acceptance is a real constraint: meta-UTAUT work on hotel-service robots and TAM/SOR studies of smart-hotel technology show that perceived usefulness, ease of use, hedonic value and resistance shape guest adoption (Marghany et al., 2025; Yang et al.; “Young luxury hotel guests…”, 2025). Second, value realisation depends on organisational readiness, not purchase: reviews of hospitality technology adoption identify persistent entry barriers alongside the added value of Technologies 4.0 (Navigating the Technological Landscape in Hospitality, 2024; Jayawardena et al., 2023).

4.4 Theme 4 — Sustainable service innovation outcomes

The outcome literature is converging on a multi-dimensional construct. Synthesising service-innovation typologies with the sustainability turn, the study identifies five outcome dimensions (see Table 2.3), and the evidence for each is now reasonably established:

  • Service-experience innovation — personalisation and digitally mediated journeys; smart-hotel studies link these to visit intentions (Yang et al.; “Young luxury hotel guests…”, 2025).
  • Process innovation — energy, water and waste efficiency, often instrumented by digital sustainability platforms.
  • Business-model innovation — partnerships and open innovation; tourism reviews confirm open innovation improves service innovation (Pinhal et al., 2025).
  • Social innovation — local sourcing, workforce development and community benefit (Elkhwesky et al., 2022).
  • Environmental/ecological innovation — footprint reduction and asset restoration (AL Mushattat et al., 2025).

Evidence also links sustainable practice to performance and loyalty: quality and sustainability practices together raise visitor satisfaction and destination loyalty (Vargová Dzurov et al., 2025), and ESG is becoming central to hospitality competitiveness and finance (Shin et al., 2026). This supports the study’s treatment of sustainability as an opportunity dimension of innovation rather than a compliance cost.

The macro picture is one of strong post-pandemic recovery with residual risk. National data show arrivals returning to and then exceeding pre-pandemic levels, and earnings recovering (Statistics Mauritius, 2026; Marques, 2026). Chapter Five

5Discussion

This chapter answers each research question in turn, refines the conceptual model, and examines the tensions that make luxury digitalisation distinctive.

5.1 Answering RQ1 — Which competencies matter?

The recent literature converges on a small, stable set of competency clusters. Crucially, the clusters are not merely technical: they combine strategic vision, analytical fluency, change capability, entrepreneurial orientation and ethical stewardship. This finding matters for hospitality because it implies that hiring purely for digital technical skill would be insufficient; the leader 4.0 is a translator between technology, people and place. It also aligns with the observation that transformation success is a leadership phenomenon rather than a technology one (Schiuma et al., 2024), and with the general consensus that digital leadership centres on communication, trust and cohesion as much as on tools (López-Figueroa et al., 2025).

5.2 Answering RQ2 — Mechanisms through which Industry 4.0 enables sustainable service innovation

Four mechanisms recur. (1) Instrumentation: sensors, meters and digital platforms make resource consumption and guest behaviour visible, turning sustainability from an aspiration into a measurable process-innovation target. (2) Personalisation: analytics and AI enable tailored experiences — the core of luxury — but do so at a scale and precision impossible manually, enabling experience innovation. (3) Integration: unified platforms dissolve data silos, allowing guest, operational and sustainability data to inform one another and enabling business-model innovation via partners. (4) Automation: robotics and contactless service free staff for high-value interaction, enabling the “human–digital balance” that luxury requires.

Because each mechanism depends on organisational capability and on acceptance, the effect of technology is contingent rather than automatic — consistent with adoption-barrier findings in hospitality and with technology-acceptance research (Jayawardena et al., 2023; Marghany et al., 2025).

5.3 Answering RQ3 — The Mauritian situation

Mauritius presents a favourable but demanding context. Demand has recovered strongly and earnings are substantial, giving resorts the financial headroom to invest. Yet the destination’s structural exposures — aviation and source-market concentration, environmental fragility, skills gaps and data-protection obligations — mean that undirected technology adoption is unlikely to be sustainable. The sector shows genuine leadership in pockets (certification, water and plastic initiatives, academic documentation of quality and sustainability practice), but the evidence base is thin and fragmented. There is, in short, demonstrated practice without a synthesised leadership framework to guide it — precisely the gap this study begins to fill.

5.4 Answering RQ4 — The refined framework

The refined model places digital leadership competencies as the human microfoundations of dynamic capabilities, with Industry 4.0 enablers operating across a layered stack and sustainable service innovation as a multi-dimensional outcome. The model’s distinctive moves are: (i) sustainability is embedded within the competency set (stewardship) rather than treated as a separate concern; (ii) guest and workforce acceptance are explicit moderators; and (iii) the outcome feeds back to competitive advantage and destination resilience, creating a renewal loop.

5.5 Tensions & paradoxes of luxury digitalisation

Three tensions run through the synthesis and deserve explicit treatment (interpretation).

The high-tech / high-touch paradox. Luxury is defined partly by human attentiveness, yet Industry 4.0 pushes toward automation. The resolution suggested by the evidence is a division of labour: automate the mundane, protect and elevate the human. Leaders who manage this balance well convert technology into more, not less, personal service — which is why “stewardship” and “vision” sit alongside “literacy” in the competency set.

The sustainability-as-cost / sustainability-as-differentiation paradox. Sustainability can be framed as compliance cost or as premium differentiation. The evidence increasingly supports the latter — sustainability practices raise satisfaction and loyalty — but capturing that value requires innovation and storytelling capability, i.e., leadership, not just operational change (Vargová Dzurov et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2026).

The island-scale paradox. Small scale is both Mauritus’s vulnerability (limited skills pool, concentrated markets, fragile ecosystems) and its strength (few, professionally managed groups; strong service culture; feasibility of coordinated certification and skill-building). Leadership competencies are the pivot on which scale-dependence turns into coordination capability.

5.6 Theoretical dialogue

The study contributes to the dynamic-capabilities tradition by specifying the human microfoundations of sensing, seizing and transforming in a service context, and by extending that tradition to explicitly include sustainability as an output rather than an externality. It also responds to calls in the digital-transformation and tourism literature for integrated, organisationally grounded accounts of technology value (Buhalis et al., 2024), and to the sustainability literature’s need for agency — the review work of Blanco-Moreno et al. (2025) and Elkhwesky et al. (2022) documents the field without naming who leads it.

Chapter Six

6Conclusion

6.1 Summary of findings

This dissertation asked how digital leadership competencies drive sustainable service innovation in luxury beach resorts in Mauritius facing Industry 4.0. Drawing on literature and secondary data published between 2021 and 2026, it found that: (1) five competency clusters consistently define digital leadership; (2) these competencies act as microfoundations of dynamic capabilities rather than as direct causes of innovation; (3) Industry 4.0 technologies enable sustainable service innovation through instrumentation, personalisation, integration and automation, but only when leadership and acceptance conditions are met; (4) sustainable service innovation is multi-dimensional and increasingly linked to loyalty and competitiveness; (5) Mauritius shows strong recovery, meaningful but fragmented practice, and distinctive structural exposures; and (6) barriers and enablers are both leadership-mediated.

6.2 Contributions

  • Theoretical. An integrated competency–capability–innovation framework that positions leadership competencies as dynamic-capability microfoundations and embeds sustainability within them.
  • Contextual. The first synthesis positioned specifically on Mauritian luxury beach resorts, linking a thin sectoral literature to the international evidence base.
  • Practical. A diagnostic of competency clusters and a barrier-mapping matrix that leadership teams can use to prioritise development and investment.
  • Agenda-setting. Six testable propositions and a clear empirical research route.

6.3 Recommendations

For educators and professional bodies: embed the five clusters in hospitality leadership curricula and professional development, using the barrier matrix as a needs-analysis tool.

6.4 Limitations

As a literature- and secondary-data study, this dissertation does not measure actual competency levels, cannot establish causality, and depends on the availability and quality of secondary evidence. English-language and publication biases are possible, corporate disclosures may be selectively positive, and the evidence window (2021–2026) means the field is still moving. The Mauritian-specific evidence base is thin, so some contextual claims rest on a limited number of sources. These limitations are material to interpretation and are the direct motivation for the agenda below.

6.5 Future research

  1. Primary empirical testing. A survey of resort executives and managers in Mauritius using validated competency, dynamic-capability, technology-acceptance and sustainable-innovation scales, analysed via PLS-SEM to test P1–P6.
  2. Comparative and multi-level designs. Compare Mauritian luxury beach resorts with peer island destinations (Maldives, Seychelles, Réunion) to isolate island effects.
  3. Qualitative case studies. In-depth study of resorts that have achieved certification to uncover competency practices and the micro-processes of change.
  4. Guest-side research. Extend acceptance research to the specific luxury/high-touch segment, examining where automation enhances versus erodes perceived luxury.
  5. Longitudinal work. Track competency development and innovation outcomes over time to move from correlation toward causal inference.

6.6 Concluding remarks

The evidence assembled here supports a clear conclusion: for Mauritian luxury beach resorts facing Industry 4.0, technology is a necessary but insufficient condition for sustainable service innovation. What converts technological capability into sustainable, differentiating innovation is leadership competence — vision, data fluency, agility, innovation orientation and, above all, responsible stewardship of guests, people and place. Digital leadership is therefore not a technical speciality but the organising capability through which island luxury hospitality can innovate sustainably and remain worthy of the environments on which it depends.

Appendix C — Glossary of key terms

  • Digital leadership: the capability to set digital direction, cultivate digital culture, make evidence-based decisions and continuously renew the organisation in a technology-rich environment.
  • Dynamic capabilities: the firm’s capacities to sense opportunities and threats, seize them, and transform the organisation accordingly (Teece, 2007).
  • Industry 4.0 / Hotel 4.0: the fusion of physical operations with cyber-physical systems, IoT, AI, big data and robotics, applied in hospitality settings.
  • Sustainable service innovation: the introduction of new or significantly improved services, processes or business models that simultaneously create guest value and environmental/social benefit.
  • Microfoundations: the individual- and team-level behaviours and competencies through which firm-level capabilities are built and enacted.
